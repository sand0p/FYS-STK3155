Here are balanced pros and cons of using neural networks (NNs) for regression:

Pros
- Captures complex nonlinear relationships: NNs approximate highly nonlinear functions and interactions without manual feature engineering (universal approximation).
- Handles high-dimensional inputs: Scales to many features (e.g., images, text embeddings, sensor arrays) where linear models struggle.
- End-to-end learning: Can learn from raw or minimally processed data; integrates feature extraction and regression in one model.
- Multi-output regression: Naturally supports predicting multiple continuous targets jointly and modeling their correlations.
- Flexible architectures: CNNs, RNNs/LSTMs, Transformers, and GNNs can exploit structure in data (spatial, temporal, sequence, graph).
- Strong regularization toolkit: Dropout, weight decay, batch norm, early stopping, data augmentation help control overfitting.
- Hardware acceleration and tooling: Mature ecosystems (PyTorch, TF), GPUs/TPUs, automatic differentiation, and model optimization frameworks.
- Transfer learning potential: Pretrained backbones (e.g., vision, language) can drastically cut data requirements and training time.

Cons
- Data hungry: Often require large, representative datasets; small datasets favor simpler models (linear/trees/Gaussian processes).
- Training complexity: Many hyperparameters (architecture, learning rates, optimizers) and potential instability (vanishing/exploding gradients, poor local minima).
- Interpretability: Hard to explain; feature importance and causal insights are less straightforward than with linear or tree-based models.
- Compute and latency cost: Training (and sometimes inference) can be expensive; may not fit edge/real-time constraints without pruning/quantization.
- Overfitting risk: Powerful capacity can memorize noise, especially with limited data or label noise.
- Poor extrapolation: NNs often interpolate well within the training distribution but can behave unpredictably outside it.
- Uncertainty estimation is nontrivial: Requires Bayesian NNs, ensembles, MC dropout, or quantile/heteroscedastic heads; adds complexity.
- Sensitivity to preprocessing: Input scaling, target normalization, and outliers significantly affect stability and performance.
- Reproducibility variance: Results can vary with random seeds, weight initialization, and nondeterministic kernels.
- Data shift vulnerability: Performance degrades under covariate or concept shift; monitoring and continual learning may be needed.

When they’re a good fit
- You have lots of data or can leverage pretrained models.
- Relationships are complex and nonlinear, and feature engineering is costly or infeasible.
- Inputs have structure (images, sequences, graphs) that specialized NN architectures can exploit.
- You can afford iterative tuning and have compute resources.

When to prefer alternatives
- Small to medium tabular datasets with mixed data types: Tree-based models (XGBoost, LightGBM) often excel with less tuning.
- Need for interpretability and calibrated uncertainty: Linear models with regularization, generalized additive models, or Gaussian processes.
- Strict resource/latency limits: Simpler models or distilled/quantized small NNs.

Practical tips if you use NNs for regression
- Normalize inputs and targets; consider standardized targets to stabilize training.
- Start simple (a few layers, ReLU/SiLU, Adam/AdamW) and add complexity only if needed.
- Use early stopping, dropout/weight decay, and validation curves to control overfitting.
- Calibrate and quantify uncertainty (e.g., deep ensembles, MC dropout, quantile loss).
- Monitor extrapolation behavior; constrain outputs if necessary (e.g., via activation or loss design).
- Consider ensembling with gradient-boosted trees for tabular data to hedge weaknesses.
