Great start. Your table nicely shows the speed–stability trade-off of fixed learning rates. Here are ideas you can add to deepen the discussion, both theoretically and practically, plus a short paragraph you can drop into your report.

What fixed learning rate guarantees for least-squares problems
- Quadratic structure: For OLS and Ridge with loss f(w) = (1/2n)||Xw − y||^2 + (λ/2)||w||^2, the Hessian is H = (1/n) X^T X + λ I (symmetric positive definite).
- Convergence condition: Gradient descent with a fixed step α converges for any initialization iff 0 < α < 2 / λ_max(H). Equivalently, α must be smaller than twice the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue of H.
- Rate and optimal fixed α: Let m = λ_min(H) and L = λ_max(H), and κ = L/m be the condition number. The optimal fixed step for quadratics is α* = 2 / (L + m), with linear convergence rate factor ((κ − 1)/(κ + 1)) per iteration. As κ grows, convergence becomes slower and the “safe” α shrinks.

What Ridge changes and why your table behaves as it does
- Ridge shifts the spectrum of H by λ: both m and L increase by λ, which reduces κ and improves the convergence rate for a given safe α. However, the strict upper bound 2/L can become slightly smaller because L increases.
- With a tiny λ = 1e−4 and high-degree polynomial features (degree 12), X^T X is typically very ill-conditioned, so κ is huge and 2/L is very small. That’s why both OLS and Ridge tolerate similar maximum α in your table and both blow up at 0.17.
- If you increase λ meaningfully, you should see a wider stability margin (easier tuning) and fewer iterations for the same tolerance, even if the theoretical α upper bound changes little.

Oscillations, overshoot, and catastrophic divergence
- Mode-wise behavior: Along an eigenvector of H with eigenvalue μ, the error contracts by a factor |1 − α μ| each step. If α μ ∈ (0, 2), that component converges; if α μ ∈ (1, 2), it flips sign each step (oscillatory convergence). If α μ ≥ 2 for any mode, the iteration diverges.
- What your negative R^2 means: The huge negative R^2 at α = 0.17 signals divergence and numerical overflow in the parameters and predictions. In practice, monitor the training loss and stop if it increases for several iterations; also consider gradient clipping or a max-norm constraint on parameters to avoid catastrophic blow-up during sweeps.

Why higher α looked “better” up to a point
- With a fixed iteration budget, larger α moves you toward the optimum faster initially, improving R^2 early—until you cross the stability boundary and explode.
- If α is close to the upper bound, you may see oscillations that slow or stall fine convergence even if the method is technically stable.

Practical ways to choose or stabilize a fixed learning rate
- Scale/precondition the data:
  - Standardize features (zero mean, unit variance) before forming polynomial features; also consider rescaling after expansion.
  - Use orthogonal polynomial bases (e.g., Legendre, Chebyshev) instead of raw monomials to improve conditioning dramatically.
- Estimate a safe α from spectral information:
  - For the loss above, L = λ_max(H) = σ_max(X)^2 / n + λ. A quick power iteration on X (or H via Hessian–vector products) gives an estimate of σ_max(X) and thus a conservative α ≈ 1/L or a more aggressive α ≈ 2/(L + m) if you also estimate m.
- Use simple line search:
  - Backtracking with an Armijo condition picks a stable α per iteration and guarantees monotone decrease of the loss, avoiding manual tuning and divergence.
- Employ learning-rate schedules:
  - If using stochastic/minibatch GD, a fixed α converges only to a neighborhood; decay schedules (e.g., α_t = α0 / (1 + k t) or exponential decay) are standard.
- Increase λ when using high-degree polynomials:
  - Stronger regularization improves conditioning (reduces κ), making fixed α more robust and speeding convergence for the same α.

Suggested additional experiments/plots
- Plot training loss vs. iterations for several α values to show fast progress, oscillations near the boundary, and divergence beyond it.
- Vary λ on a grid and show how the maximum stable α and the iteration count to reach a target loss change with λ (illustrating the conditioning effect of Ridge).
- Estimate L via power iteration and overlay the theoretical stability limit α < 2/L on your empirical sweep.
- Compare standardized vs. unstandardized features and orthogonal vs. monomial bases at the same α.

Optional aside: alternatives to plain GD
- For these convex quadratic problems, conjugate gradients or direct solvers via SVD/QR are often far more efficient and don’t require step-size tuning. This can be a useful baseline to mention.
